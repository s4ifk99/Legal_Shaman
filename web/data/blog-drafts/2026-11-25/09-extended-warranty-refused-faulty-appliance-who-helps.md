## Summary

If an **extended warranty** is refused on a **faulty appliance**, you may still have rights against the seller under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. Those statutory rights sit beside any warranty, and they cannot be signed away. Who helps: write to the seller and the warranty provider first, then your card issuer, then the Financial Ombudsman if the warranty is an insurance contract or a bank was involved. ## Key Information

- The seller must supply goods of satisfactory quality, fit for purpose and as described. An extended warranty is extra. It does not replace those rights.
- Within 30 days of delivery you can reject a faulty item for a full refund. After that, the seller gets one chance to repair or replace. If that fails, you can ask for a refund or a price reduction.
- A fault that appears within six months is assumed to have been there at delivery unless the seller proves otherwise.
- Read the warranty: some are insurance contracts (the Financial Ombudsman may be able to look at a refused claim), and some are only a promise by the trader.
- If you paid by card, ask about chargeback or **Section 75**.

## What the law says in England and Wales

This section explains the general law. It is not advice on your situation.

- Under the **Consumer Rights Act 2015**, goods bought from a business must be of satisfactory quality, fit for the purpose and as described. These rights last for up to six years in England and Wales, though you must still show the fault was there at delivery after the first six months.
- A trader cannot use an extended warranty, or a "no refunds" term, to cut down those statutory rights.
- An extended warranty is a separate contract. If it is arranged as insurance, complaints can usually go to the insurer and then the Financial Ombudsman Service. If it is only the trader's own promise, your complaint stays with the trader, then advice services or court.
- **Section 75** may apply to credit card purchases between £100 and £30,000.

## Practical Guidance

- Write to the **seller** describing the fault and asking for a repair, replacement or refund under the **Consumer Rights Act 2015**, even if the warranty was refused.
- In parallel, complain to the **warranty provider** in writing and ask whether the warranty is an insurance contract.
- If you paid by card, ask your card issuer about **chargeback** or **Section 75**.
- If an insurer or bank refuses, complete their complaint process, then the **Financial Ombudsman Service**.

## FAQ

**The manufacturer warranty has ended. Do I have any rights left?**

Yes, possibly, against the seller under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, if you can show the fault was present when the goods were supplied. An extended warranty is a separate route, not the only one.

**The warranty company says the fault is wear and tear.**

Ask for that in writing and still pursue the seller under the statutory rights if the appliance failed earlier than you would reasonably expect. A second opinion on the fault can help.

**Is the Financial Ombudsman always available?**

Usually only if the warranty is insurance, or if a bank, insurer or other financial firm is involved. A trader's own warranty may not be in the ombudsman's remit.
